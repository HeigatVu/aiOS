# B. PROJECT SUMMARY
This project addresses the growing prevalence of children’s developmental language and speech sound disorders by leveraging artificial intelligence to support the assessment process and personalized therapy.
Recent estimates suggest that approximately 20% of children in Vietnam are affected by these disorders. Current therapeutic approaches require direct interaction between children and therapists, while at-home practice is often considered supplementary and has limited impact on treatment outcomes. Compounding this challenge is the severe shortage of speech-language pathologists, with fewer than 100 specialists per 100 million people in Vietnam, leading to high workloads for therapists and limited access to care. These factors underscore the urgent need for scalable, technology-driven solutions to mitigate the current constraints in speech and language therapy. To address these challenges, this project proposes an AI-powered system containing a dedicated AI proxy device, computer-based software, and a mobile application. This system is designed to support clinicians in assessing and treating children with developmental language disorders by providing personalized therapy plans, enabling effective at-home practice, and continuously monitoring progress. By reducing the burden on specialists and improving accessibility to care, particularly in underserved areas, this solution has the potential for broad deployment across Vietnam, with an initial focus on hospitals in Ho Chi Minh City, where a higher concentration of patients facilitates early implementation and evaluation.
The intellectual merit of this project lies in its novel application of AI techniques to enhance speech and language therapy. Specifically, the project aims to develop AI models capable of simulating speech and language patterns of children with developmental disorders, analyzing their responses, and generating personalized treatment recommendations. The system will employ real-time adaptation based on individual progress, offering a transformative approach to diagnosis and therapy. Additionally, the AI proxy device, designed to operate modern AI models efficiently, will connect with therapists’ computers to provide real-time analysis and treatment recommendations. To support this, we will also develop dedicated computer-based software that integrates with the AI proxy device, assisting therapists in evaluating disorder severity and generating personalized intervention strategies. Complementing this, a mobile application will leverage AI to deliver therapist-guided, personalized exercises for at-home practice, ensuring continuity of care beyond clinical settings.
The broader impacts of this project are substantial and multifaceted. First, it improves access to speech and language therapy, making it suitable for rural areas and adaptable to different Vietnamese dialects when trained with more diverse Vietnamese language data. Second, the project can create a new therapy paradigm by integrating AI-driven tools that not only enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of treatment but redefine therapeutic approaches, allowing clinicians to focus on complex cases while automating routine tasks. Third, the adaptability of the AI-powered system extends beyond speech and language therapy, with potential applications in other fields requiring structured exercises, such as physical therapy or education, like language learning. Finally, by creating a scalable and adaptable solution, the project can serve as a model for other countries facing similar challenges, contributing to global efforts to improve pediatric healthcare outcomes.
# C. PROJECT DESCRIPTION
## 1. Specific Aims
This project aims to develop an AI-driven system that enhances speech and language therapy for children with developmental language disorders through assessment, personalized treatment, and progress tracking. Our approach consists of three key components:
AI Proxy Device & Clinician Software: We will develop a hardware-assisted AI proxy device that runs AI models locally to assist clinicians in evaluating speech and language disorders. This system will be integrated with computer-based software to simulate children’s speech patterns, provide data-driven assessments, and personalize treatment recommendations.
Mobile Application for Children: To extend therapy beyond clinical settings, we will design a mobile application that delivers AI-driven, personalized exercises tailored to each child’s unique speech and language challenges. This app will monitor children’s practice sessions, track their progress, and dynamically adjust exercises to match their evolving needs.
Digitalized Therapy Management: By integrating AI into both assessment and treatment, we aim to streamline clinicians’ workflows, enable precise progress tracking, and optimize treatment regimens over time. The system will facilitate real-time updates to therapy plans, ensuring that interventions remain effective and adaptable to each child’s development.
## 2. Background and Significance
Development of language and speech in children: Language development is a complex process shaped by biological, cognitive, and environmental factors [54]. By 10–12 months, children typically begin recognizing phonemes of their native language, saying first words, and using meaningful gestures like pointing. Around 18 months, their vocabulary may reach 50 words, with frequent gesture-vocalization combinations. Between 20 and 20–24 months, after acquiring about 100 words, children experience a “vocabulary spurt” and begin forming two-word sentences [54]. By 36 months, with a richer vocabulary, they start producing grammatically complex sentences and can communicate with people outside their family. However, about 11–18% of toddlers aged 18–36 months show delayed lexical development and are referred to as “late talkers,” despite having no neurological, intellectual, or hearing impairments. These delays can affect both expressive and receptive language. While many late talkers improve significantly after age 3 and function within typical language bounds, some may continue to face challenges in daily communication [55].
Developmental language disorder: While many late talkers improve after age three, a subset continues to struggle and may be diagnosed with Developmental Language Disorder (DLD) [55]. According to the CATALISE Consensus, DLD refers to language impairments that arise developmentally and are not caused by biomedical conditions such as brain injury, neurodegenerative diseases, cerebral palsy, or genetic syndromes [56]. The consensus expands diagnostic criteria to include children with significant language impairments regardless of risk factors, co-occurring conditions, or nonverbal IQ [56]. In the UK, DLD affects about 7.6% of children aged 4–5, higher than the prevalence of Autism Spectrum Disorder (1.76%) [58] and ADHD (5%) [59]. Children with DLD often face serious challenges, particularly in preschool and early primary school. For 40–50%, language difficulties also affect thinking and learning processes [68], becoming more apparent as they begin learning to read and write by connecting sounds to letters [69]. DLD also increases the risk, about five times, of facing additional difficulties such as academic struggles, behavioral or mental health issues, emotional regulation problems, and social challenges [68].
Speech sound disorder: Speech sound disorder (SSD) refers to difficulties with producing speech sounds, typically due to two main issues. One is articulation deficits, which are motor-based problems where the child’s mouth, tongue, or lips do not move correctly to produce certain sounds [60, 61]. The other is phonological deficits, which are knowledge-based issues involving difficulty understanding and using the sound rules of a language. In such cases, the child may produce sounds correctly in isolation but misuse them in words according to predictable patterns [60, 61]. SSD differs from language impairments like DLD as it specifically concerns speech sounds [60]. It is one of the most common communication disorders in children, affecting about 8–9% of preschoolers and 2–3% of school-aged children [61]. It is more prevalent in boys and in groups such as children with ear infections or language delays [61, 62]. SSD can seriously impact communication, academic performance, and social-emotional well-being, lowering quality of life [63]. Academic struggles are common, especially in reading and writing, and are more severe when SSD co-occurs with DLD, as children may struggle to follow classroom instructions [63].
Assessment for language and speech sound disorders in children:
For developmental language disorder: There are three main types of assessment. Norm-referenced assessments (standardized tests) compare a child's abilities to those of a representative peer group, offering a snapshot of performance at a given time. These are primarily used to identify potential language impairments [64, 65]. Criterion-referenced assessments evaluate a child's performance against specific skill benchmarks rather than peer comparisons. They help determine what a child can or cannot do, establish intervention targets, and monitor therapy progress over time [64, 65]. Dynamic assessment (DA) focuses on learning potential through a test-teach-retest model. After an initial test, the clinician provides targeted instruction, then retests to assess how well the child learns from the intervention. DA is especially useful for culturally diverse populations and helps distinguish true language impairments from limited exposure or other factors [64].
For Speech sound disorder: For children with SSD, a full speech sound assessment is time-consuming, so the process often begins with a quick screening to identify those who may need further evaluation [66, 67]. There are two types: informal screening, which involves tasks like conversation or reading simple sentences, judged by the examiner; and formal screening, which uses standardized tools with set procedures, often comparing results to age norms or cutoffs [66]. While screening is efficient, it does not provide a diagnosis or determine therapy needs. A comprehensive assessment offers deeper insight and includes [66, 67]: connected speech sampling (speech during conversation/storytelling), single-word testing (using picture-naming to assess specific sounds), error analysis (to detect phonological patterns), stimulability testing (checking if a child can produce sounds correctly with a model), severity and intelligibility ratings (judging speech impact and clarity), and speech perception testing (assessing ability to distinguish similar sounds).
Traditional intervention:
For developmental language disorder: The interventions for children with DLD aged 3 - 5 years aim to enhance expressive and receptive language, promote effective social communication, and build foundational literacy skills [65]. Although typical milestones guide potential goals, the range is extensive. Therefore, clinicians must carefully prioritize targets, balancing developmental sequences with individual factors like the child’s profile, age, severity, and family priorities for focused and relevant therapy [65].
For speech sound disorder: The main goal of SSD treatment is to help individuals produce sounds correctly and use them automatically in everyday speech [67]. The general treatment steps for this disorder include establishment (teaching children how to produce the target sound correctly), generalization (helping them use the sound correctly in sentences or conversation), and maintenance (making the correct production consistent and automatic) [67]. The therapist will select initial sounds or patterns to treat based on different factors: developmental, intelligibility, complexity, stimulability, and client-specific [67].
Technical Background:
Why personalized therapy?: As mentioned above, current therapy for children with language and speech sound disorders primarily relies on direct, in-person sessions with trained therapists. The frequency of these sessions varies depending on the severity of the disorder but typically involves at least 1–2 sessions per week because the daily intervention schedule does not suit typical speech-language pathology practices, and dividing up sessions across weeks is more beneficial than scheduling them all in one week [70]. However, due to a shortage of qualified therapists, many are overwhelmed with high caseloads and unable to provide thorough, individualized care for every child. Digital and personalized therapy presents a promising solution to this challenge. Initial efforts to bring computer-assisted approaches to personalized therapy began as early as 2008 [3–6], but limited technological capabilities stalled progress, creating a need for a new wave of AI-enabled approaches to revitalize the field.
Current technical solutions: A key limitation in developing personalized therapy systems is the challenge of accurately analyzing complex speech symptoms and simulating disorder progression to guide treatment planning [17, 18]. With recent breakthroughs in artificial intelligence, particularly the rise of large language models, personalized medicine has gained renewed attention as a transformative research direction [7, 8]. Language and speech sound disorders are no exception, with a growing number of AI-driven applications aimed at helping children improve their communication skills [9]. However, most existing tools function as standalone solutions without the involvement of a real therapist, which limits their effectiveness [9]. Our proposed approach (detailed in the following section) introduces a novel therapy paradigm. It includes simulated disorder profiling by a multimodal large language model, adaptive assessment, personalized long-term intervention planning, and AI-edge deployment.
Language model usage in education: Evidence from the education domain reinforces the feasibility of this approach, demonstrating how LLMs can effectively simulate learner behavior to support teaching and evaluation.  Monforte et al. [12], for instance, developed a peer agent that generated realistic collaborative interactions, while other studies showed how LLMs can simulate student responses at varying skill levels to evaluate educational content [13–16]. These findings suggest that LLMs are capable not only of modeling diverse learner behaviors but also of providing tailored, pedagogically relevant interactions.
Language model usage in healthcare: In the healthcare domain, LLM-based systems have been used to support history-taking and clinical decision-making training by emulating patient responses and providing structured feedback. Recent studies demonstrate that AI-generated feedback can align with expert human evaluations and enhance clinical reasoning skills in students [17–19]. Moreover, recent work highlights the potential for using LLMs to simulate diverse patient demographics, making medical education more inclusive and accessible [20–22]. Mental health tools like VirtuAlz [23] and PATIENT-𝜓 [24] further show how cognitive behavior models can be integrated into AI simulations, offering a compelling foundation for adapting similar techniques to pediatric speech-language therapy contexts. Although LLMs have been widely studied in the medical domain, their practical deployment demands careful consideration. The deployed system must be evaluated rigorously to ensure safety, fairness, and transparency. [25] emphasize the lack of standardized evaluations, while others raise concerns about algorithmic bias, privacy, and ethical risks of anthropomorphizing AI agents, especially in contexts involving vulnerable populations like children [26, 27].
Adaptive learning and assessment: Central to delivering effective AI-assisted therapy is the ability to assess learners adaptively over time. Adaptive assessment, widely used in education and healthcare, allows for efficient and personalized evaluation or personalized medicine through intelligent item selection [33, 36]. Underpinning this approach is Item Response Theory (IRT), which models the relationship between a learner’s ability and item characteristics to drive Computerized Adaptive Testing (CAT) [28]. Innovations in IRT—such as memory-based cycles, nonparametric models, and integration with tutoring—have enabled increasingly personalized assessment systems [28–35].
Long-term planning techniques: While IRT-based approaches are powerful for assessment, personalized treatment planning requires more sophisticated long-term decision-making. Non-myopic Bayesian optimization (BO) addresses this need by planning multiple steps ahead, making it particularly well-suited for adaptive speech-language therapy [38–40]. Unlike myopic methods that focus on immediate gain, non-myopic BO methods consider how present actions shape future outcomes. Techniques like GLASSES, BINOCULARS, and rollout-based policies offer efficient strategies for long-horizon planning under uncertainty [41–45], and recent efforts integrate reinforcement learning to adapt interventions directly from observed data [44–46]. These capabilities allow non-myopic BO to dynamically adapt therapeutic interventions over time, optimize treatment plans under cost or time constraints, and ultimately lead to more effective and individualized care in speech-language therapy.
Edge deployment for AI models: To make these AI-driven strategies truly accessible and responsive, edge deployment is essential for scaling up our solution. Recent advances in edge computing and edge AI enable LLMs and optimization-based methods to run directly on resource-constrained edge devices [47, 48, 50]. This paradigm reduces reliance on centralized infrastructure, thereby improving latency, privacy, and cost-effectiveness, which are crucial for healthcare contexts where data sensitivity is paramount [47, 50, 53]. By deploying AI on edge proxies, our system ensures real-time, localized decision-making and assessment, bringing trustworthy and scalable speech-language therapy directly to affected children.

## 4. Approaches

Figure 1: System architecture of our proposed solution. The AI proxy device, equipped with an embedded NVIDIA AI module, processes real-time speech data captured by a multidirectional microphone. The AI-analysed results are transmitted to the therapist’s PC or laptop, where a computer-based software assists in assessment and treatment planning. Data is stored and synchronized via a cloud server, enabling a mobile software on a patient tablet to deliver personalized therapy exercises and track progress remotely.
The investigation will be conducted in four phases, each designed to systematically develop, validate, and implement an AI-driven system for personalized speech and language therapy for children with developmental language disorders. Our solution mainly includes the AI proxy devices, computer-based software for therapists, and a mobile application for patients. An overview of the system architecture of our solution is presented in Figure 1.
Our target is to enhance the current therapy paradigm with the aid of AI techniques. Specifically, the treatment process begins when the child first visits the clinic, where the therapist utilizes the AI proxy device and computer-based software to conduct initial speech and language assessments. The child will take a language test and/or a speech test, depending on their disorder status, on the therapist's computer. The test results, including speech data collected from the multidirectional microphone, will be used to create a surrogate agent that simulates the child's speech and language disorder. Next, we utilize AI-based planning algorithms to analyze multiple treatment scenarios, aiming to select the treatment plans where the surrogate agent shows the most improvement. These plans will be presented to the therapist to assist them in making more personalized treatment decisions for the child.

Figure 2: The proposed therapy paradigm. In clinical settings, the child takes a language and/or speech test on the therapist’s PC/laptop, with speech data captured by a multidirectional microphone. The system leverages the AI proxy device to generate personalized treatment plans, which are reviewed and finalized by the therapist before synchronization to the cloud server. In at-home settings, the child practices AI-personalized exercises on a patient tablet, supervised by parents. Practice results are automatically uploaded to the cloud, enabling therapists to track progress and refine treatment plans in subsequent clinical visits.
After the clinical sessions, the child continues therapy at home using a mobile application on a tablet or smartphone, which delivers personalized exercises aligned with the recommended treatment plan. The exercises can be delivered under gamified activities, enhancing children's engagement during practice. Over time, progress data collected from home-based practice is synchronized to the cloud server and automatically reported to the clinician for review during the next visit. During follow-up visits, the clinician reviews this data, conducts reassessments using the AI proxy device and computer-based software, and adjusts the therapy plan accordingly. This cycle is repeated until the child's speech and language abilities reach age-appropriate norms. We visualize the overall progress of the new therapy paradigm in Figure 2.
We propose three phases to implement our solution: (i) data collection and preparation, developing an AI proxy device and an adaptive assessment method, (ii) developing a personalized method and applications, and (iii) real-world implementation and evaluation. Phase 1 lays the foundation by collecting and preprocessing essential speech and language data from both children with developmental speech-language disorders and typically developing children. This data serves as the backbone for AI models and assessment methods are developed. In this phase, the AI proxy device will also be built. In phase 2, multimodal learning models will be trained to simulate children’s language-speech development, enabling adaptive assessment and personalized treatment recommendations. These models are then refined in the next step, where personalization techniques are applied to tailor treatment plans to individual children. The insights from Phase 2 are used to develop both computer software and mobile applications for integrating AI techniques in real-world use. Finally, in Phase 3, the system is deployed and evaluated in clinical settings, where its effectiveness is compared against traditional therapy methods. The details of these phases are presented below.
Phase 1: Data Collection, preparation and developing an AI Proxy Device and an Adaptive Assessment Method (12 months)
This phase focuses on gathering data and understanding existing treatment and assessment methods for developmental speech and language disorders in children aged 3 – 6. Particularly, we intend to collect disordered children’s demographic information, language-speech assessment results, speech samples, and details of the treatment process, including but not limited to therapist decisions and taken exercises. Collected data will be preprocessed and stored for use in subsequent phases. This phase also involves the development of the AI proxy device.
P1.1: Collect and conduct a comprehensive review of current treatment and assessment methodologies for developmental speech and language disorders. This will involve collaborating with speech-language pathologists, collecting and digitizing clinical guidelines, and reviewing literature.
P1.2: Collect data from at least 150 children aged 3-6 diagnosed with speech sound and developmental language disorders at the children's hospital of Ho Chi Minh City.
P1.3: Collect data from at least 150 normal developing children aged 3-6 from kindergartens after undergoing specific tests from doctors, then collecting demographic information, language-speech test results, and speech samples. The data from these children will be used to jointly build the children's simulation and as the control group to evaluate the children's improvements.

P1.4: Develop the AI proxy device, which will serve as the interface for data collection and AI model deployment. Specifically, we intend to build a processing board integrated with an NVIDIA Jetson Orin module with at least 100 TOPS. This board will connect to the multidirectional microphone and the therapist’s computer through USB connections. The role of this AI proxy is to execute speech-collecting commands, simulate children, and analyze the language-speech assessment responses to provide personalized treatment suggestions for therapists.
P1.5: Analyze the assessment questions and children's responses from collected tests and exercises in P1.1, using Item Response Theory (IRT) [28]. This phase aims to find question/exercise difficulty and relevance information, including question discrimination, guessing factors, and facilitating efficient assessments in later phases.
P1.6: Develop an adaptive method to evaluate disorder level based on children’s responses (including language and speech) during practice and assessment phases. This will involve analyzing behavioral and performance data based on measurement theory using techniques such as IRT, maximizing Fisher information in psychometrics [28]. In particular, this adaptive method will be integrated into both the therapist’s computer software and the patient’s mobile application. On the therapist's side, this method helps to evaluate disorder level with as few tests as possible, saving time for clinic periods. On the patient side, using the assessment results, our system will automatically generate suitable exercises for children based on expert knowledge.
Phase 2: Developing multimodal models, building a personalized method and applications (16 months)
This phase focuses on developing multimodal models for simulating children with language-speech developmental disorders and the method and applications for personalizing treatment and adaptive assessment for children with developmental speech-language disorders.
P2.1.1: Develop a multimodal large language model (MLLM) that integrates both speech and text data to simulate a child during the treatment process. The model takes input of  a history of previous questions or exercises, the child's corresponding responses, and a new question. By analyzing the prior questions and responses, the model generates an appropriate response to the new question. To accomplish this, the model must be capable of both receiving and generating text and speech simultaneously, which poses a technical challenge in the current research landscape. This model will be trained on datasets from P1.2 and P1.3 and validated against clinician assessments.
P2.1: Develop methods for personalizing treatment plans:
P2.1.2: Develop an adaptation strategy to adapt the general simulation model (P2.1.1) for an individual child. In detail, we intend to use a child’s responses and speech samples (P1.2 and P1.3) to fine-tune a small part of the MLLM using transfer learning techniques such as Low-rank Adaptation. Data augmentation techniques will be implemented to enhance the diversity of children's data across diverse demographics and disorder levels. The fine-tuning process will be performed locally on the AI proxy device (P1.4) in under 5 minutes. We also implement periodic evaluation while fine-tuning to ensure the model well reflects the child’s current disorder level.
P2.1.3: Develop an optimization method utilizing IRT-based question analysis techniques and results (P1.5) to construct treatment plans for maximizing children's improvement in speech-language skills.
P2.1.4: Integrate the developed adaptive assessment method (P1.6) into the pipeline to evaluate the children periodically at each clinic visit.
P2.1.5: Develop a recommendation method for at-home practice exercises based on domain knowledge and each child’s progress. This method will also incorporate the adaptive assessment method (P1.6) to simultaneously evaluate children’s improvement while practicing.
P2.1.6: Evaluate the entire personalization pipeline and compare the results with those from traditional therapies using the collected data (P1.2). This phase also involves speech-language therapists in assessing the recommended treatment plans generated by our method to determine their appropriateness and to identify potential personalized treatment decisions that might have been overlooked.
P2.2: Refine the optimization method (P1.5, P2.1.3) based on feedback from speech-language pathologists. We expect to receive feedback and adjust the appropriateness of the optimization objective, the length of optimization periods, and any relevant factors that should be considered.
P2.3: Refine the assessment method (P1.6, P2.1.4) based on feedback from speech-language pathologists. We expect to receive feedback and adjust the minimum number of questions in the assessment, the factors that contribute to the disorder level, and the comprehensiveness of the evaluation metrics used.
P2.4: Develop a computer-based application for therapists to conduct language and speech assessments and integrate P2.1.2–P2.1.4 to recommend personalized treatment plans. The optimization techniques (P2.1.3) will try to recommend plans that can improve the child's simulated responses the most. The software allows therapists to revise assessment results, modify the most suitable treatment plan, before making the final decision for each child.
P2.5: Develop a mobile application integrating P2.1.3 and P2.1.5 for use by children and their parents. This application will retrieve the therapist's decisions from the cloud server and provide an at-home practice plan with personalized exercises for children.
Phase 3: Real-World Implementation and Evaluation (9 months)
This phase involves deploying the system in clinical settings and evaluating its effectiveness.
P3.1: Conduct a comparative study between two groups of children with developmental speech-language disorder, one using traditional therapy methods and the other using a personalized AI-driven system. Metrics such as treatment duration, improvement rates, and clinician workload will be compared. Statistical methods (e.g., t-tests, ANOVA) will be used to compare outcomes between the two groups.
P3.2: Refine assessment and personalization methods based on real-world feedback and data. Qualitative feedback from clinicians and parents will be analyzed thematically to identify strengths and areas for improvement.
New Methodology and Advantages: The project introduces several innovative methodologies:
AI proxy device for efficient model deployment (P1.4): This device enables local deployment of AI techniques, fostering large-scale deployment of our solution.
Multimodal language model (P2.1.1, P2.1.2): The model combines speech and text data as input and output to simulate children during treatment processes, enabling more accurate and personalized therapy planning.
Adaptive assessment utilizing IRT (P1.5, P1.6): This technique makes the assessment process more efficient, reducing time consumption and overhead workload on therapists.
Personalized treatment plan with nonmyopic decision-making techniques (P1.5, P2.1.3): Optimizes long-term treatment outcomes, ensuring efficient and effective interventions.
Personalized at-home exercises (P1.6, P2.1.5): With this approach, the frequency of clinic visits can be reduced while ensuring continuous improvement in children. This helps save time and money for each visit and reduces the workload on therapists.
The project faces several potential technical difficulties and limitations that need to be addressed. First, ensuring sufficient high-quality data for training LLMs, particularly for rare or complex cases, poses a significant challenge. Second, achieving model generalization for diverse populations of children from 3 to 6 years old in the south of Vietnam and variation between boys and girls in speech sound and developmental language disorders is critical, as models must perform effectively beyond the collected datasets they were trained on. Third, ethical and privacy concerns are paramount, requiring robust safeguards to protect sensitive patient information, including personal and speech data, in compliance with privacy regulations. Finally, user adoption presents a hurdle, as therapists and parents must feel comfortable and confident in using the new technology. Addressing these challenges will be essential for the successful implementation and scalability of this solution.
If technical challenges arise, one alternative option is to adopt hybrid models, which combine AI-driven assessments with periodic therapist oversight to strike a balance between automation and human expertise. Another option is implementing simpler rule-based systems for initial speech-language assessments, though this may come at the cost of reduced personalization. Additionally,  teletherapy integration could be used, incorporating remote therapy sessions led by experienced clinicians to complement AI-driven tools and provide a more comprehensive treatment experience. In cases where the selected hardware lacks sufficient VRAM or computational speed, techniques such as model simplification–removing unimportant parameters or employing model compression methods–can be applied to ensure efficient deployment without significantly compromising performance.
## 5. Innovative Technology
This project leverages advanced technologies to support treatment for developmental speech and language disorders in children. Below, we outline the key innovations, their application in the project, and their technical novelties.
1. AI Proxy as the Central Processor: An AI proxy device will serve as the central processor for running multimodal simulations, analyzing assessment and therapy outcomes, and recommending personalized treatment plans. Designed to ease clinicians' workload, it automates routine tasks and delivers actionable, data-driven insights. With real-time data processing, the AI proxy can be integrated into clinical workflows, enabling timely and informed decision-making without deep technical expertise.
2. Multimodal Large Language Models for Patient Simulation: This project develops a multimodal large language model (MLLM) that integrates speech and text to simulate children with language and speech sound disorders. The MLLM analyzes speech patterns, vocabulary, grammar, and fluency to answer test questions, helping therapists predict outcomes and refine therapy plans. This is the first time MLLM has been used to simulate individuals with speech sound and developmental language disorders. Moreover, we intend to use transfer learning for efficient adaptation to each child, reducing computation-intensive scratch training, while real-time updates ensure therapy recommendations remain personalized and responsive to ongoing assessments and at-home exercises.
3. Item Response Theory (IRT) and Adaptive Assessment: We will use IRT to analyze assessment questions and exercises based on difficulty, discrimination, and guessing factors, enabling more accurate insights into a child's abilities. Building on this, an adaptive assessment method will select the most informative questions to maximize accuracy while minimizing effort, resulting in a more efficient and personalized evaluation process for children.
4. Nonmyopic Decision-Making for Long-Term Therapy Optimization: Nonmyopic decision-making algorithms will be used to optimize long-term treatment outcomes. These algorithms prioritize long-term treatment outcomes rather than short-term gains, ensuring that therapy decisions contribute to sustained progress gradually. Moreover, they are integrated with AI models, combining insights from the multimodal LLM and IRT-based assessments to create a comprehensive, forward-looking therapy plan tailored to each child's needs.

Equipment costs:
Based on our prior experience in training language models [73], an adequate GPU memory requirement for effective model training is approximately 160 GB of VRAM. The a2-highgpu-2g machine on Google Cloud meets this requirement, as it is equipped with 2×NVIDIA A100 80GB GPUs, 24 vCPUs, and 170 GiB of RAM. This machine is priced at approximately 5,364.14 USD per month under standard on-demand pricing. In addition to the computing configuration, we require a 256 GB bootable disk for storing the operating system, and 4×375 GB additional disk storage for saving trained models and datasets. These additional storage requirements bring the total monthly cost to approximately 5,508.74 USD. This estimate is based on Google's official pricing calculator (https://cloud.google.com/products/calculator).
According to the accounting exchange rate published by the Ministry of Finance in April 2025, where 1 USD = 24,786 VND, the monthly cost for Google Cloud computing translates to approximately 136.54 million VND.
This cloud server will primarily be used in Phase 2.1.1 (2 months) for training multimodal large language models, and Phase 2.1.2 (1 month) for testing and validating the entire pipeline. Hence, the estimated total duration of usage is three months, leading to a total projected cost of 409.62 million VND for Google Cloud computing services during the project.
